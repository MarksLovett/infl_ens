The setup

A pool of LoRA adapters over one frozen base model. Each adapter occupies a position in a seven-dimensional trait space; an incoming prompt is embedded to a point in that same space; and the adapter that answers is chosen by how much of the "resource" at that point each adapter commands. The allocation rule and the dynamics come from the influencer's game, so the adapters' positions are not hyperparameters — they are the equilibrium of a game the adapters play against each other over the prompt distribution.

Three numbers recur throughout. Pooled is one generalist adapter trained on all the same data — the honest baseline. Router is what the learned routing achieves. Oracle is what perfect per-prompt routing of the same adapters would achieve; it is a ceiling conditional on the ensemble, not on perfection. From these:

gain = pooled − router what routing won envelope = pooled − oracle what was there to win captured = gain / envelope the share the router actually took

Separating gain from envelope matters more than it looks. They move independently, and nearly every result below turns on the difference between "there was nothing to route between" and "there was, and the router missed it".

How the data is split

Seven safety benchmarks, one per trait axis, partitioned 70/10/20 per benchmark into train, validation and test. The partition is frozen in a manifest and shared by every arm of an experiment, which is what makes the arms data-matched.

PartitionPromptsUse
train19,848the twelve closed-loop rounds
validation2,836per-round eval at rounds 4 and final
test5,671every number in this document; never seen in training

Rounds are sized so that training covers the train partition exactly once: 1,654 prompts × 12 rounds = 19,848. The round schedule is a without-replacement shuffle, so no prompt is seen twice and none is skipped. Within a round, the batch is routed, each pair's adapter runs one SFT pass on what it was given, and each clone takes one position step. Under fully dense soft routing every pair sees every prompt, weighted by its share.

Two details worth stating because they are easy to assume otherwise. The SFT loss is full-sequence causal LM over the chat-formatted prompt and response — only padding is masked, so prompt tokens are supervised too, not just the completion. And the evaluation NLL uses the identical token scope, so the training objective and the reported metric measure the same thing.

What a seed changes

There are two independent seeds and conflating them causes real mistakes, so they are worth separating explicitly.

SeedWhat it controlsConsequence
run seed the random start of the non-convex equilibrium solve; the per-round batch order; stochastic routing draws; LoRA init and dropout selects which basin the solve lands in — i.e. which axis each pair ends up owning. The equilibrium radius is stable to about 0.5%; the assignment of pairs to axes is not.
data-split seed the 70/10/20 partition itself a different train pool and a different test pool

A dseed replicate moves both together, which is the bound that supports a generalisation claim; a run-seed-only replicate would only bound retraining reproducibility. Each data split trains its own generalist — sharing one across splits would score specialists against a baseline fitted to different data.

The consequence that shapes every cross-split figure: absolute NLL is not comparable across data splits, because each has its own test pool. The generalists sit at 2.0151, 1.9841, 1.9925 and 1.9991 across the four completed splits — differences of the same order as the effects being measured. Only within-split contrasts carry across, which is why every replicate figure plots improvement over that split's own generalist rather than raw NLL.

Trait space and the encoder

The seven axes are not chosen by hand; each is learned from one benchmark that supplies positive and negative examples. In benchmark order the axes are harm (BeaverTails), hallucination (HaluEval), jailbreak (JBB-Behaviors), privacy (AI4Privacy), over-refusal (OR-Bench), injection (prompt-injection) and policy violation (Do-Not-Answer). That order is the coordinate order everywhere.

The pipeline from a prompt to a point:

prompt → encoder embedding → per-axis Fisher direction (shrinkage, Gram–Schmidt) → robust 5/95 affine calibration → mode-alignment blend → coordinate residualisation → quantile/CDF normalisation → b* ∈ [0,1]⁷

The CDF stage is always on and is what guarantees the box: each axis is mapped through its own empirical CDF, so the calibration marginals come out near-uniform by construction. Measured on the 28,355-prompt corpus, per-axis means are 0.49–0.50 and standard deviations 0.289–0.291 against the uniform ideal of 0.2887.

The resource density B(b) is a Gaussian KDE of those coordinates on a coarse lattice — three points per axis, so 3⁷ = 2,187 cells. Only the density and the grid enter the game mathematics; the continuous coordinate is what routing actually uses.

Left: the density of the 28,355-prompt corpus on three axis pairs, darker where prompts concentrate. Right: each pair's final coordinate on all seven axes. The pairs sit inside the mass rather than at the corners of the box — they are competing for prompts that exist, not tiling the space.

Swapping the encoder

This is deliberately a one-line change: the trait-space config includes an encoder fragment, and swapping that fragment is the whole intervention. Nothing in the source changes. What follows from it:

ChangesDoes not change
cache fingerprint and directory; a full re-encode of the corpus; embedding dimension; every learned direction, calibration bound, residualiser and CDF knot; the density; σ₀* (weakly); any stored positions the grid; the number and order of axes; the split manifest; the entire learning algorithm; the [0,1]⁷ box with near-uniform marginals; all game mathematics; the σ policy; the theory init; everything downstream
σ₀* is close to encoder-invariant, and that is a property of the design rather than luck. The lattice is pinned to {0, 0.5, 1}⁷ and the CDF pins each marginal to near-uniform, so the resource covariance can only move within a narrow band: its eigenvalues are 0.141–0.182 against the exactly-uniform value of 0.167, putting Λmax just 9% above the lattice bound. A different encoder perturbs the threshold; it cannot move it by a large factor.
One trap worth knowing before the encoder experiments. The cache fingerprint hashes the encoder's model id, but not its pooling, maximum length or normalisation — all of which do change the geometry. Change those and the fingerprint stays put, so a stale cache is silently reused. Redirect the cache directory when varying them.

The theory initialisation

Adapters do not start at random positions. They start at the equilibrium of the game, computed before any training:

  1. Draw 14 clones uniformly in the box (a minimum-separation constraint exists but never binds in seven dimensions — the measured start already has minimum pairwise distance 0.46).
  2. Run simultaneous projected gradient ascent, each clone on its own utility, for up to 8,000 steps.
  3. Pair the clones by nearest neighbour and co-locate each pair at its centroid. Nearest-neighbour, not harm-order: in seven dimensions the partners are not adjacent along any single axis.
  4. Re-run the ascent from the paired state. This second pass provably cannot separate a pair — co-located clones see identical allocations, so they receive identical gradients forever. Co-location is an outcome the theory predicts, not a constraint imposed on the loop.

The competitive reach is set relative to the stability threshold:

σ₀* = √( (N−2)/(N−1) · Λ_max(Σ_B) ) = 0.40977 for N = 14 σ = 0.50 · σ₀* = 0.20489

Below σ₀* the symmetric configuration — every adapter stacked at the centre of mass — is unstable along the principal axis of the resource covariance. Every arm here sits deliberately below it, at half the critical reach, because that is the regime in which specialists separate at all.

Two diagnostics that look alarming and are not. The runs report layout = collapsed and converged = False. The first is a legacy label: the classifier sorts along the harm axis alone and was written for a four-agent study in two dimensions. The actual final spread is 0.825, well above its own collapse threshold of 0.45, and the pairs occupy six distinct dominant axes. The second means only that the maximum coordinate change had not fallen below 10⁻⁸ after 8,000 steps — a tolerance artefact at N = 14, not divergence. The solve is bitwise reproducible across processes.

Routing: hard, soft, top-k, unit

Everything rests on one allocation rule. With a shared multivariate-Gaussian kernel of width σ, adapter i at position xi commands a share of the prompt at b:

G_i(x, b) = f_i(x_i, b) / Σ_j f_j(x_j, b) log f_i = −½ (x_i − b)ᵀ Σ⁻¹ (x_i − b), Σ = σ²I

σ is therefore the temperature of a softmax over adapters. Small σ approaches winner-take-all; large σ drives every share toward 1/N.

Hard versus soft

Hard routing draws one winner per prompt from the categorical distribution G. One prompt trains exactly one adapter, at unit weight. Soft routing draws nothing: the prompt trains several adapters at once, each weighted by its share. The routing unit is the co-located pair, whose share is the sum of its members' — which equals the share a single adapter would have at that shared position, but only because every group is the same size. That is why the code requires groups of exactly two.

Top-k and the loss weighting

These are two separate knobs, and the five-arm ladder exists to separate them. Top-k decides which pairs see a prompt: keep the k largest shares and renormalise. When k equals the number of pairs, nothing is truncated and the allocation is untouched. The loss weighting decides how hard each of them is pushed: weighted multiplies each example's loss by that pair's renormalised share, so a prompt a pair barely claims contributes a correspondingly small gradient; unit trains every selected pair as if it fully owned the prompt. The membership is identical either way — only the gradient magnitude differs, and neither touches the position update.

Why the position update matches the theory

The loop never takes a gradient step. It moves each adapter part-way toward a weighted centroid of the prompts it saw:

x̂_i = Σ_m G_i(1−G_i) b_m / Σ_m G_i(1−G_i) (weighted centroid of the batch) x_i ← (1−β) x_i + β x̂_i (β = 0.5)

And the utility gradient the theory prescribes is:

∇_{x_i} u_i = Σ_b B(b) · G_i(1−G_i) · Σ⁻¹ (b − x_i)

The two are the same direction. The centroid's drift is a positively weighted average of (b − xi) with exactly the coefficient Gi(1−Gi) that appears in the gradient, and for an isotropic kernel Σ⁻¹ is a positive scalar that changes the magnitude but not the direction. That is what "theory-matched" means, and it is checked numerically in the test suite at cosine similarity above 0.9999.

Under hard routing the sampling step already supplies one factor of Gi, so the centroid only needs to carry (1−Gi) to complete the coefficient. Soft routing samples nothing, so it carries the whole product — and can do so over the entire batch rather than just the prompts a pair trained on, which is the lower-variance form of the same estimator.

One prediction of this falls out cleanly and is worth recording, because it is measured rather than enforced. Two clones sharing a position receive identical allocations, hence identical gradients, hence identical updates — co-location is a fixed point of the dynamics. The loop therefore never constrains partners to stay together; it only records the distance between them. Under soft routing that distance is exactly zero at every round of every run. Under hard routing, which samples a single winner and so breaks the symmetry, the partners drift apart by 0.05–0.11. The theory says which of those should happen, and it is what happens.

The headline above — that hard k=1 loses to its own generalist — is a property of the expected metric, not of the adapters. Scored as an ensemble the same arm gains +0.0341 and captures 62% of its envelope. The experts it trained were fine; committing to one of them was what cost it.

That is the same mechanism the reach sweep shows at long σ, in its most extreme form. Hard routing trains each expert on a single categorical draw, so the experts are noisier and the argmax is least trustworthy — exactly the regime where choosing badly is expensive and averaging is free. Note also that the ladder's spread compresses: on the expected metric it runs −0.016 to +0.069, crossing zero; on the mixture it runs +0.034 to +0.110, a 3.2× ratio with no sign change. Routing design still matters and dense soft still wins, but by less than the expected metric suggests.

The router's weights are earned by the loss weighting, not the gate. The G-vs-uniform margin is +0.043 for both weighted arms, but only +0.028 for two of the three unit arms. Share-weighted training is the setting in which the loss ever sees the allocation, and it is what makes G informative at evaluation time.

Result: competitive reach

The single most robust finding in the sweep is at the long-reach end: σ = 0.70 is the worst setting in every split on every column, and it has the tightest error bar of any row. Everything from 0.20 to 0.50 is a plateau whose rows are not separable from one another at n = 4.

Do not read a peak off this curve. The best-performing σ is different in every one of the four splits — 0.20, 0.30, 0.50 and 0.40 respectively. A seed-0 spike at σ = 0.20 was what originally motivated running replicates at all, and as a spike it did not reproduce: σ = 0.20 is the argmax only on seed 0, where it sits at z = +2.47 against its own split, versus +0.35 to +0.87 elsewhere. Two different failures that look alike

Read the oracle − pooled and captured columns of the table above together, row by row. The router column alone conflates two failures with opposite causes:

  • Long reach — a routing failure. At σ = 0.70 the envelope is unremarkable, sitting near its sweep-typical value: the specialists did separate, and the headroom is there. Capture falls to roughly a third, the lowest in the sweep. The router simply cannot tell the specialists apart — which the argmax-agreement column corroborates, also bottoming out on this row.
  • Short reach — a specialisation failure. At σ = 0.05 capture is ordinary, mid-range for the sweep. What has collapsed is the envelope itself, the smallest in the table. The router worked as well as it does anywhere; there was simply less to route to.

Both show up as a smaller number in the router − pooled column, which is why that column is not diagnostic on its own. This is the whole reason the envelope is carried as a column rather than left implicit.

Result: how many pairs

{{fig:pair_count}}

Eight ensembles from 2 to 9 pairs, all at seed 0 against one shared generalist, so absolute NLL is comparable here. There is a threshold between four and five pairs: the gain nearly triples and capture jumps from 33% to 62%. This is a step, not a slope.

Below the threshold, routing is at best marginal and at two pairs actively harmful — the router loses 0.0011 nats to the generalist. What makes this informative rather than merely negative is that the envelope grows smoothly across the whole range while capture does not: at two, three and four pairs the specialisation is there and the router fails to exploit it.

Seven pairs — the configuration the whole replicate sweep is running at — is the worst of the five arms above the threshold. Six pairs gives 0.0818 gain at 63.7% capture against seven's 0.0692 at 53.5%, with essentially the same envelope. Five, six, eight and nine pairs all beat it, which makes a sampling artefact less likely than a genuine local minimum. This is n = 1 and wants a second split before it is leaned on, but it bears on where the remaining GPU time should go.

Eight and nine pairs put more routing units than there are trait axes, and nothing degrades: the envelope keeps growing and capture holds near 55%. The axis count is not an upper bound on useful pairs.

The mixture confirms the threshold independently

Rescoring every pair-count arm as an ensemble reproduces the same step without sharing arithmetic with the expected metric beyond the underlying NLL matrix: the equilibrium start is worth a mean +0.0209 in mixture gain at K ≤ 4 and −0.0019 at K ≥ 5.

The two regimes have different bottlenecks. What the router's weights are worth over uniform averaging of the same experts rises monotonically with pair count — from +0.0009 at K = 2 to +0.0495 at K = 9. So starting position matters when pairs are scarce, and the weights matter when pairs are plentiful. At K = 2 from a naive start the weights buy essentially nothing; the equilibrium start lifts that twelvefold, to +0.0112. By K = 6 the two initialisations are indistinguishable on this measure too, because with enough experts the router has something real to discriminate between wherever they started.

How far the pairs leave the theory solve, and why that is the useful part

Every arm so far begins from a naive start. The theory solve maximises ui = Σb B(b) Gi(b) over agent positions, so the answer it gives depends entirely on what B(b) — the resource measure — says about where prompts live. In every run reported above, B carries almost no information about that: it is close to a uniform prior over the {0, 0.5, 1}7 lattice, per-axis variance 0.159 and anisotropy 1.29, against the prompt cloud's actual 0.084 and 3.10.

The reason is mechanical. _kde_on_grid subtracts a per-grid-point maximum for log-space stability and never multiplies it back, so each grid point's density is divided by its own nearest-prompt kernel value — the dominant term. A single prompt returns a uniform vector wherever it sits; ninety prompts at one corner and ten at another return [0.45, 0.50, 0.05], putting the most weight on the location with no prompts at all.

That makes the existing runs a well-defined baseline rather than a spoiled one: agents started from an uninformative prior over trait space. The natural comparison is against agents started from an equilibrium of the same game solved against the real prompt distribution — which is the ablation the next section runs. Nothing here is invalidated: training always drew its position updates from real prompt batches, so only the starting point and the σ₀* normalising constant came from the naive measure.

Each panel therefore carries three outlines. The grey dashed one is the approximate initialisation the run started from. The red dash-dot one is the same game solved against the empirical prompt cloud — where the theory would have placed that pair had it seen the data. The solid coloured one is where twelve rounds of routed training actually left it. Two gaps matter, and they are different questions: how far training moved from where it started, and how far it finished from where the theory points.

Would more rounds have closed the gap?

This is answerable without a GPU and without training anything. The position update never looks at an adapter — under soft routing it is just

x̂_i = Σ_m G_i(1−G_i) b_m / Σ_m G_i(1−G_i), x_i ← (1−β) x_i + β x̂_i

which depends only on the prompt coordinates and σ. So the trajectory can be continued on CPU from wherever training stopped. And the fixed point of that update, x̂i = xi, is algebraically the same condition as ∇xiui = 0, because the gradient is σ−2 Σm Gi(1−Gi)(bm − xi). Continuing the loop therefore has to land on an equilibrium of the game under the empirical measure. The question is which one.

Three things follow, and the third is the interesting one.

  • Twelve rounds is nowhere near converged. The gradient at round 11 is large in every arm. Training stops well short of a fixed point.
  • The loop does converge if you let it — 100 to 260 further rounds reaches ‖∇u‖ < 10−3, and it costs seconds of CPU. But the positions barely move getting there: 0.03 to 0.12.
  • It converges to a different equilibrium than the fresh solve. The distance to the empirical-B solve is essentially unchanged by the continuation — 0.350 → 0.344, 0.294 → 0.294. At σ = 0.50 the pairs move a mean of 0.24 in L2 across four splits, with individual pairs between 0.09 and 0.44, in a space whose diagonal is √7 ≈ 2.65. So roughly a tenth of the space — far more than a rounding error, and far less than a wholesale reassignment. Three things about that movement are systematic across every split and every reach we have scored:

  • It is inward, always. The mean radius of the configuration about its own centroid shrinks in every run measured — 0.585 → 0.518 at σ = 0.50 and 0.510 → 0.357 at σ = 0.70, both on split 0. The theory solve places the pairs more spread out than training sustains.
  • Much of it is retreat along the pair's own dominant axis. Of the 0.24 mean displacement at σ = 0.50, −0.06 is the pair backing away from the very coordinate it specialises in; at σ = 0.70 that rises to −0.13 of 0.27, about half the total. Specialists moderate their own extreme rather than sharpening it. This is what the contraction is made of.
  • The direction is consistent across axes. Averaged over four splits at σ = 0.50, injection (+0.013) and policy (+0.004) gain coordinate while harm (−0.028) and hallucination (−0.021) lose it. Every split shows the same signs on harm and injection.

That last ordering is suggestively the same one as the per-benchmark gains below — the axes pairs drift toward are the axes routing pays on, and the axis they retreat from hardest, harm, is the one that is flat everywhere. The rank correlation over the seven axes is ρ = +0.64, but with seven points that is p = 0.12 and not significant. It is a hypothesis the encoder experiments can test properly, not a result.

Why any of this is interpretable. A pair's position is seven numbers in [0,1] on axes that were named before training started — harm, hallucination, jailbreak, privacy, over-refusal, injection, policy. There is no probing step and no post-hoc labelling of experts: to ask what an expert does, you read its coordinates. That makes two otherwise-invisible things checkable. First, the theory's prediction and the trained outcome live in the same coordinates, so "the solve was wrong about this pair, in this direction, by this much" is a sentence with numbers in it. Second, the drift is a measurement of the landscape: a pair that moves toward injection is reporting that the prompt mass it actually won was more injection-heavy than the lattice model predicted.

One cheap diagnostic falls out of this. Ask whether a pair's argmax axis is still the one the theory assigned it after training. At σ = 0.50 it usually is — 7/7, 7/7, 6/7 and 5/7 across the four splits. At σ = 0.05 it usually is not — 4/7, 5/7, 5/7, 3/7. Short reach does not merely compress the specialists, it reassigns them, which is a sharper statement of the envelope collapse than the NLL table can make. Note that σ = 0.70 retains identity perfectly respectably (4/7, 5/7, 5/7, 6/7) while capturing the least of any setting — exactly the signature of a routing failure rather than a specialisation failure, and an independent confirmation of that split.

Result: naive start vs the game equilibrium

The whole pair-count ablation, rerun from an equilibrium of the influencer game solved against the empirical prompt cloud instead of from the naive uniform prior. One factor moves. Same split, same σ, same twelve rounds, same dense soft routing, same pooled generalist — only where the agents start.

{{fig:naive_vs_theory}}

Upper: routing gain under each initialisation, with the oracle ceilings behind in thin blue. Lower: their difference, against the ±2σ band measured from the arms where the two agree. Higher is better in both. Shaded region marks the regime below the original threshold.

The effect is a step function, not a trend. Below the threshold the equilibrium start is worth +0.026, +0.013 and +0.036 nats at K = 2, 3, 4. Above it the five arms scatter around zero — −0.005, +0.001, +0.003, −0.003, +0.004 — with mean −0.000 and SD 0.0039. Those five nulls are what the error band is built from, so they serve as a measured noise floor rather than an assumed one: the three effects below the threshold are 6.6σ, 3.3σ and 9.2σ against it.

Where the agents start decides what they become — but only when capacity is scarce. With five or more pairs the game reaches equivalent specialisation from either starting point. With four or fewer it does not, and the naive start leaves real performance unclaimed. At K = 2 it is the difference between routing hurting (−0.0011) and helping (+0.0247).

The envelope column says which half of the pipeline is responsible, and it splits the same way. Below the threshold the oracle ceiling itself rises — +0.020, +0.007, +0.018 — so the equilibrium start produces genuinely more separated specialists, not merely better exploitation of the same ones. Above it the ceiling moves by less than 0.001 in either direction. Nothing is created where capacity was already sufficient.

It also relocates the threshold

The original ablation's jump sits between K = 4 (+0.027) and K = 5 (+0.075), which is where the dotted line comes from. Started at the equilibrium, K = 4 reaches +0.0635 — most of the way to what the naive start needed five pairs to achieve. So part of "you need at least five pairs" was a property of the initialisation rather than of the method.

The equilibrium is a fixed point, and the agents stay in it

The position update never consults an adapter, so its behaviour is checkable directly. Arms started at the equilibrium move a mean of 0.013 over twelve rounds with straightness 0.12 — below the 0.30 expected of a random walk, meaning consecutive steps are anti-correlated and the agents are jittering in place on batch noise. Step size is flat from round 1 to round 12 rather than decaying, which is the signature of a noise floor rather than of convergence. Arms started naively travel 0.25 with straightness 0.66–0.85: directed motion toward somewhere else.

That is the theory's own prediction, confirmed. The update's fixed point, x̂i = xi, is algebraically the condition ∇xiui = 0, so an agent placed at an equilibrium has no reason to move and demonstrably does not.

The same contrast across competitive reach

The pair-count ablation above moves capacity. This moves reach instead: all ten σ settings, each trained from both starting points. It is the one comparison in the study that is genuinely paired — every arm on both sides scores the identical 5,671-prompt test partition against the identical pooled generalist at 2.0152 nats, verified cell by cell. The usual "absolute NLL is not comparable" caveat does not apply, and the difference column is a within-prompt contrast rather than a cross-split one.

The headline is that the equilibrium does not raise the mean. Across the ten reaches the mean difference on the expected metric is +0.0007. It wins at eight of ten, but the two it loses include the largest single difference in the sweep — −0.0215 at σ = 0.20 — and that one cell cancels the other eight. The median is +0.0027.

Reach 0.20 is not an outlier, and that matters

The tempting move is to call σ = 0.20 a fluke of seed 0 and quote the median. The replicate data says otherwise. The naive reach sweep has four data splits, and σ = 0.20 beats its own split's mean in all four:

So the naive start really is good at this reach — the sign replicates four times out of four. What fails to replicate is the size: seed 0's excess is roughly three times the other three splits'. And the equilibrium arm at σ = 0.20 sits essentially at its own sweep mean (+0.0702 against +0.0705), so the entire −0.0215 is a naive bump, not an equilibrium dip.

The consequence is a correction to the obvious reading of the table. On a typical split the difference at σ = 0.20 would be nearer −0.008 than −0.0215, which would put the mean difference around +0.002 — close to the median. The median is the better summary of that column, but not because σ = 0.20 is noise to be discarded. It is because seed 0 happens to sample the high tail of a real effect. Discarding the cell would overstate the equilibrium; taking seed 0's version of it at face value understates it.

The advantage is specific to committing to one expert. Under sequence-level mixing the equilibrium is ahead at only four of ten reaches, with mean difference −0.0019. Mixing already recovers most of what a mis-placed specialist costs, so a better starting geometry buys least exactly where the combination rule is most forgiving. Reported as a single number over both metrics, this result would disappear.

What changes is the shape of the reach response, not its level

The reproducible difference is not where the curves sit but how they move. The natural summary — standard deviation across reach — gets this wrong, and it is worth seeing why, because it is the kind of error that survives review. The mixture gain genuinely trends upward with reach under both starting points: softer allocation mixes better even as it selects worse. SD charges both curves for that real trend, lands at ≈0.0156 either way, and reports no difference at all.

The second difference annihilates any straight line, so mean |second difference| along the reach axis measures zigzag rather than spread. On that statistic the two families separate on both metrics (σ = 0.70 excluded throughout — its equilibrium is degenerate):

Why I do not yet attribute that smoothness to the equilibrium

There is a second explanation for the same numbers, and the current data cannot rule it out. The two sweeps do not start from comparable places. Every naive arm uses init_mode: theory_gradient_paired, which re-solves the theory initialisation from a fresh random start at each σ against the near-uniform reward; the equilibrium arms use init_mode: given with positions solved against the empirical reward. Measuring how far the initial pair positions move between adjacent reaches — Hungarian-matched, mean L2 in the 7-D unit box:

initial positions, adjacent reachesmedian step maxsteps under 0.05
naive0.3020.460 0 of 9
equilibrium0.0140.178 7 of 9

A ~20× difference, and the configurations differ in kind, not only in distance. Counting the set of trait axes the pairs occupy at initialisation, the naive sweep produces six distinct axis-sets across ten arms — σ = 0.05 covers privacy and over-refusal but not jailbreak or policy, σ = 0.10 swaps those, σ = 0.30 carries both jailbreak and privacy but drops over-refusal. The equilibrium sweep produces four, and eight of the ten share a single set, with σ = 0.10 through 0.55 identical. The naive sweep is not one configuration observed at ten reaches; it is ten configurations indexed by reach.

So the smooth-family reading is at least as well supported as the equilibrium reading, and the obvious bridge between them fails: if basin-hopping drove the zigzag, larger position steps should produce larger gain changes, and within the naive family they do not. Spearman ρ = −0.40 (p = 0.29, n = 9) — the wrong sign. Within the equilibrium family ρ = +0.67 (p = 0.050); pooled across both, ρ = +0.39 (p = 0.11). A family-level association with n = 2 families and no within-family dose-response is not a mechanism.

The roughness ratio may be a fact about how the two sweeps were built rather than about equilibrium. One family is a smooth one-parameter family by construction; the other re-solves independently at every reach. The control that separates them is cheap to state and has not been run: repeat the naive sweep holding positions fixed across σ — take one naive solve and reuse it at all ten reaches, changing only σ. If the naive curve smooths, the result is about construction. If it stays rough, it is about equilibrium. Until then the roughness numbers stand as measured and the causal reading does not.

A related gap: there are no run-seed replicates anywhere in the study — no arm has been retrained at a second run seed on the same split. So training-run noise at fixed (σ, initialisation) is unmeasured, and the +0.0027 median sits on top of an unknown. Two arms at σ = 0.50, run seed 1, would size it, and would test the mechanism directly besides: the run seed moves the naive positions but leaves the equilibrium positions pinned, so the two families should respond to it very differently if the mechanism is real.

One observation survives all of that caveating, because it does not depend on which family is smoother. Neither initialisation ever occupies all seven axes, both always cover harm, hallucination, injection and policy, and privacy is the axis most often left empty — the naive sweep places a specialist there at three of ten reaches, the equilibrium sweep at two. That is the same benchmark that carries a negative gain everywhere in the per-benchmark breakdown below. The game declines to specialise on privacy, and on the rare occasions it does, that benchmark still loses.

Every point here is one data split, and ±0.004 overstates the precision. That figure is the spread of Δ across the arms where the two initialisations agree — it measures how closely two runs that landed in similar places track each other, and it is not a general bound on how far a Δ could move on a different split. A paired comparison cancels luck the two arms share (same test pool, same generalist) but not luck that depends on where the specialists ended up, and the two initialisations produce different specialists.

The size of that residual is measurable, because the naive reach sweep has three replicate splits. Seed 0's gain deviates from its own replicate mean by −0.016 to +0.013 depending on σ, with no consistent sign — and the replicate means are a flat plateau from σ = 0.20 to 0.50, meaning most of the structure in the seed-0 reach curve is split noise rather than signal.

So: the below-threshold pair-count effects (+0.013 to +0.036) survive even that larger uncertainty and I would defend them. Anything at the ±0.01 level on a single split — including the above-threshold nulls, and any individual σ comparison — is not resolved. A second data split is the cheap fix, and it is what the replicate machinery already exists to do.

That second split is now running. The ten naive dseed1 reach arms already existed, so only the equilibrium side needed training. The first arm to land, σ = 0.05, gives +0.0761 against its naive dseed1 counterpart's +0.0570 — a difference of +0.0191, where the same comparison on seed 0 gave +0.0029. But read that carefully rather than as a win: the equilibrium value sits essentially at the naive dseed1 mean of +0.0777, and the gap opens because naive σ = 0.05 was the weakest arm on that split, 0.021 below its own split's mean. One point, carried by the naive side. The claim to test as the remaining nine land is the smoothness one, and it is pre-registered here: roughness below 0.006 and SD below 0.004 for the equilibrium dseed1 curve, against 0.00813 and 0.0095 for the naive dseed1 curve that is already complete.
The σ = 0.70 equilibrium is degenerate. Two pairs merge exactly, leaving five distinct positions for seven pairs. Co-located distinct pairs receive identical allocations, hence identical updates, so the degeneracy is a fixed point — measured to hold at distance 0.000000 at every one of the twelve rounds. That arm is a five-expert ensemble carrying seven adapters and must be read as such.

The ensemble number we should be reporting

Every "router" number above is Σp Gp · NLLp — the expected loss of sampling one expert. That is the right metric for a system that must commit to a single adapter, and it is what the code has always reported. But it is not an ensemble, and if the method is described as one, the mixture is the number a reader will ask for:

mixture_i = −(1/n_i) · logsumexp_p ( log w_p − n_i · NLL_{p,i} )

By Jensen's inequality the mixture is never worse, so the ordering oracle ≤ mixture ≤ expected holds by construction. Reporting the expected value understates the method.

The machinery for this is now in place — the evaluator computes both the share-weighted mixture and a uniform-weight control, and persists the per-prompt matrix so any future weighting is an offline recomputation rather than another pass over every adapter.

{{fig:ensemble}}

Every panel is plotted as improvement over that arm's own pooled generalist, higher is better, and the oracle line is the ceiling. This is mandatory for panels (d) and (e), which span three tokenizers whose per-token NLL cannot be compared at all. The shaded band between the expected and mixture series is the Jensen gap. Panels marked pending re-score are waiting on GPU time — the expected and oracle series in them are final, and only the two mixture series are missing.

The re-score is queued across both cards behind the running replicates. It is inference only, roughly 17 minutes per arm, and does not retrain anything: the adapters are unchanged and only the scoring pass is repeated with the matrix persisted this time.

A prediction this section got wrong. An earlier version argued that because the weights enter the mixture only as −log w / n, and the mean sequence is 67 tokens, uniform and share-weighted mixing would land close together — making sequence-level mixture a weak test of routing. That is refuted. Uniform is worse in every arm scored, by 0.011 to 0.045 nats, and at seven and eight pairs it is worse than even the expected metric. The approximation from mean sequence length was too crude; the logsumexp is not as dominated by the best expert as it implied. Sequence-level mixture turns out to be a sharp test, and the router passes it.

Reporting the mixture raises the headline by roughly 50% — at seven pairs, +0.0726 becomes +0.1092, and capture goes from 56% to 85% of the envelope. The ordering oracle ≤ mixture ≤ expected holds on every run scored, which is the theorem check that the implementation is right.

The naive start is also where the router's weights stop mattering. At K = 2 from a naive start, mixture (+0.0157) and uniform (+0.0149) are indistinguishable — G-weighting contributes 0.0008, essentially nothing. From the equilibrium start the same comparison opens to 0.0112. So badly-placed specialists are no better weighted by G than averaged uniformly; well-placed ones are. That is the uniform control doing exactly the job it was added for.

It also changes where the reach sweep peaks

Under the expected metric the σ sweep peaks at 0.20 and collapses at long reach (+0.0371 at σ = 0.70). Under the mixture it peaks at σ = 0.60 (+0.1139) and 0.70 does not collapse at all — +0.1021, 82% capture. The reason is geometric: minimum pair separation falls monotonically with reach, from 0.600 at σ = 0.05 to 0.172 at 0.60 and 0.000 at 0.70, where two pairs merge exactly. Identical positions give identical G, so committing to one expert becomes a coin flip between duplicates — while averaging over them costs nothing. The capture collapse, the mixture's immunity to it, and the equilibrium degeneracy are one phenomenon, not three.
